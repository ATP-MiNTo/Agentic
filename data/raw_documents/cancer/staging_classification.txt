TNM staging system
- TNM is a standard system for describing how far a cancer has spread.
- T means the size and extent of the primary tumor.
- N means whether nearby lymph nodes contain cancer.
- M means whether the cancer has spread to distant parts of the body.
- TX means the primary tumor cannot be assessed.
- T0 means there is no evidence of a primary tumor.
- Tis means carcinoma in situ, which is early cancer limited to the original cell layer.
- T1 through T4 mean increasing size or local spread of the tumor.
- NX means nearby lymph nodes cannot be assessed.
- N0 means no cancer is found in nearby lymph nodes.
- N1 through N3 mean increasing spread to nearby lymph nodes.
- M0 means no distant metastasis is detected.
- M1 means the cancer has spread to distant organs or tissues.
- TNM is often written as a combination such as T2N1M0.
- In that example, the tumor is moderately sized, there is lymph node involvement, and there is no distant metastasis.
- TNM is especially used for solid tumors.

Stage 0 to stage 4
- Stage 0 is also called carcinoma in situ.
- Stage 0 means abnormal cells are present but have not spread into nearby tissue.
- Stage 1 means the cancer is small and confined to the original site.
- Stage 1 usually means there is little or no spread to nearby lymph nodes.
- Stage 2 means the tumor is larger than stage 1 or has started to spread into nearby tissue or a small number of nearby lymph nodes.
- Stage 3 means the cancer has grown deeper into nearby tissues and may involve more nearby lymph nodes, but it has not spread to distant organs.
- Stage 4 means the cancer has spread to distant parts of the body.
- Stage 4 is also called metastatic cancer.
- Stage 4 cancer can spread to organs such as the lungs, liver, bones, or brain.

How staging affects treatment
- Stage 0 and stage 1 cancers are often treated with local treatments such as surgery, radiation, or both.
- Stage 2 and stage 3 cancers often need a combination of treatments such as surgery, chemotherapy, radiation, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy.
- Stage 4 cancers usually need systemic therapy such as chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or targeted therapy.
- Staging also helps doctors estimate prognosis, plan follow-up, and decide whether the goal is cure, long-term control, or symptom relief.

Grading vs staging
- Staging describes how much cancer is in the body and where it has spread.
- Grading describes how abnormal the cancer cells look under the microscope.
- Lower-grade cancers look more like normal cells.
- Higher-grade cancers look more abnormal and are often more aggressive.

Why accurate staging matters
- Accurate staging helps doctors choose the right treatment.
- Under-staging can lead to treatment that is too weak.
- Over-staging can lead to more intensive treatment than needed.
- Accurate staging helps estimate prognosis and compare outcomes across patients and centers.
- It also helps identify clinical trials that match the true extent of disease.
