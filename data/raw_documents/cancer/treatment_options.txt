Surgery
- Surgery can remove the whole tumor, remove part of a tumor to reduce symptoms, or take tissue for diagnosis and staging.
- Surgery is a local treatment and works best for solid tumors in one area.
- Curative surgery aims to remove all visible cancer.
- Debulking surgery removes as much cancer as possible when full removal is not possible.
- Palliative surgery helps relieve pain, pressure, bleeding, or blockage.
- Diagnostic or staging surgery helps confirm cancer and see how far it has spread.
- Surgery is often combined with chemotherapy, radiation, immunotherapy, or targeted therapy.
- Surgery is usually not enough by itself for cancers that have spread widely.

Chemotherapy
- Chemotherapy uses drugs to kill cancer cells or stop them from growing and dividing.
- It targets rapidly dividing cells and can shrink tumors or slow cancer spread.
- Chemotherapy may be given before surgery, after surgery, together with radiation, or to control cancer that has spread.
- Common side effects include nausea, vomiting, fatigue, mouth sores, hair loss, low blood counts, infection risk, constipation or diarrhea, and nerve tingling.
- If a tumor is too large for surgery at first, chemotherapy may be used first to make it smaller.

Radiation therapy
- Radiation therapy uses high-energy radiation, usually X-rays, to kill cancer cells or stop them from growing.
- It damages the DNA of cancer cells so they can no longer divide normally.
- Radiation may be the main treatment, or it may be used before surgery, after surgery, together with chemotherapy, or to relieve symptoms.
- External beam radiation comes from a machine outside the body.
- Internal radiation therapy, also called brachytherapy, places radioactive material inside or near the tumor.
- Common side effects depend on the treatment area and may include skin irritation, tiredness, appetite loss, nausea, and local tissue inflammation.

Immunotherapy
- Immunotherapy helps the body’s immune system recognize and attack cancer cells.
- It can boost immune activity, remove the brakes on immune cells, or give the immune system agents that help it attack cancer more effectively.
- Main types include checkpoint inhibitors, CAR-T or T-cell therapy, monoclonal antibodies, and cancer vaccines.
- Immunotherapy is often used for cancers that have spread, come back, or do not respond well to other treatments.
- Side effects can include fatigue, skin changes, flu-like symptoms, and inflammation in organs such as the lungs, liver, intestines, or thyroid.

Targeted therapy
- Targeted therapy uses drugs to attack specific molecules, genes, or proteins that help cancer cells grow and spread.
- It is designed to hit a more specific cancer feature than standard chemotherapy.
- Targeted therapy may block growth signals, stop tumor cells from dividing, or cut off blood vessel growth.
- It is often used when the cancer has a known biomarker or mutation.
- Main types include monoclonal antibodies and small-molecule drugs.
- Common side effects include skin problems, diarrhea, and high blood pressure.
- Not every cancer has a targetable mutation, and some cancers can develop resistance over time.

Combination treatments
- Combination treatments use two or more cancer therapies together.
- Combining treatments can improve effectiveness, reduce resistance, and treat both local and spread disease.
- Neoadjuvant therapy is given before surgery to shrink a tumor.
- Adjuvant therapy is given after surgery to kill any remaining cancer cells.
- Concurrent therapy gives treatments at the same time, such as chemotherapy with radiation.
- Combination treatment can be more effective, but it may also cause more side effects.

Clinical trials
- Clinical trials are research studies in people that test new ways to prevent, detect, diagnose, or treat cancer.
- They help determine whether a new treatment is safe and effective.
- Main types include treatment trials, prevention trials, screening or diagnostic trials, and supportive care trials.
- Trial phases usually move from safety and dose testing to efficacy testing and then comparison with standard care.
- Clinical trials are not only for people with no other options.
