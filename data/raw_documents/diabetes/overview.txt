Definition of Diabetes
- Diabetes is a chronic disease that occurs when the body cannot properly regulate blood sugar levels.
- This happens when the body does not produce enough insulin or cannot effectively use the insulin it produces.
- High blood sugar levels over time can lead to serious health complications affecting many parts of the body.

Type 1 vs Type 2 Differences
- Type 1 diabetes is an autoimmune condition in which the immune system attacks the insulin-producing cells in the pancreas.
- People with Type 1 diabetes usually require insulin therapy for life.
- Type 2 diabetes occurs when the body becomes resistant to insulin or does not produce enough insulin.
- Type 2 diabetes is more common and is often linked to lifestyle factors such as obesity and lack of physical activity.

Risk Factors
- Family history can increase the risk of developing diabetes.
- Obesity, unhealthy diet, and physical inactivity are common risk factors.
- High blood pressure and age can also increase risk.
- For Type 1 diabetes, genetics and autoimmune factors may play an important role.

Statistics and Prevalence
- Diabetes affects millions of people worldwide and continues to increase each year.
- Type 2 diabetes accounts for the majority of diabetes cases.
- The growing prevalence of diabetes is associated with changes in lifestyle, aging populations, and increasing obesity rates.

Impact on the Body
- Diabetes can affect many organs and systems in the body.
- High blood sugar levels may damage blood vessels, nerves, kidneys, eyes, and the heart.
- If not properly managed, diabetes can lead to cardiovascular disease, kidney failure, nerve damage, and vision problems.

# Additional Information
Definition of Diabetes

Diabetes mellitus refers to a group of diseases that affect how the body uses blood glucose (blood sugar). Glucose is an essential source of energy for the cells that make up muscles, tissues, and the brain.

When the body cannot properly use glucose, blood sugar levels become too high, which may lead to serious health complications.

⸻
Types of Diabetes

Diabetes can be classified into chronic and potentially reversible conditions.
Chronic conditions

Type 1 diabetes

Type 2 diabetes
Potentially reversible conditions

Prediabetes

Gestational diabetes

Prediabetes is a condition where blood sugar levels are higher than normal but not high enough to be diagnosed as diabetes. Without intervention, it may progress to type 2 diabetes. Gestational diabetes occurs during pregnancy and often resolves after delivery.

⸻
Early Symptoms of Diabetes

Symptoms vary depending on blood sugar levels, and some individuals—especially those with type 2 diabetes or prediabetes—may have no symptoms at all.

Common symptoms include:

Increased thirst

Frequent urination

Unexplained weight loss

Fatigue or weakness

Irritability or mood changes

Blurred vision

Slow-healing wounds

Frequent infections (skin, gum, or vaginal infections)

An important clinical sign is the presence of ketones in urine, which occurs when the body breaks down fat and muscle due to insufficient insulin.

⸻
Causes of Diabetes

The exact cause of most types of diabetes is not fully known. However, genetic and environmental factors may contribute.

How insulin works:

Insulin is produced by the pancreas

It helps glucose enter the cells for energy

It lowers blood glucose levels

When insulin production is insufficient or the body becomes resistant to insulin, glucose builds up in the bloodstream, leading to diabetes.

⸻
Risk Factors

Risk factors vary depending on the type of diabetes:

Family history of diabetes

Overweight or obesity

Certain ethnic groups (Black, Hispanic, Asian American, Native American)

Environmental factors (especially in type 1 diabetes)

History of prediabetes or gestational diabetes

⸻
Complications of Diabetes

If left uncontrolled, diabetes can lead to serious long-term complications.
Cardiovascular disease

Heart disease

Stroke

Atherosclerosis (narrowing of blood vessels)
Nerve damage (diabetic neuropathy)

Tingling, numbness, or pain in extremities

Digestive problems

Erectile dysfunction
Kidney damage (diabetic nephropathy)

Damage to kidney filtering system, potentially leading to kidney failure
Eye damage (diabetic retinopathy)
